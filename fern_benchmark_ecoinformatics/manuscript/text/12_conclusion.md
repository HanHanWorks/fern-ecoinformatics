# 11. Conclusion

The question this paper opened with was whether a benchmark accuracy can serve as an estimate of field performance. On this benchmark the answer is no, and the distance between the two is measurable rather than mysterious. One model trained on both image regimes reaches 91.9% on herbarium sheets and 92.5% on field photographs; separate the regimes between training and test and accuracy falls to 42 to 65%; withhold one region and a single step costs 39.8 percentage points. None of those losses appears in the number a conventionally split benchmark reports, and each is large enough to reverse a ranking. The three assumptions that keep them hidden have each been tested here, and each fails.

Against the four questions posed in Section 2: biology-pretrained features with a frozen trunk and a linear head at 336 px are the best recipe, at 91.4 ± 0.7% over three seeds, statistically indistinguishable from full fine-tuning and +7.1 ± 1.0 pp ahead of a general-purpose self-supervised backbone (RQ1). Withholding image domain, data source or region costs 8.6 to 48.1 pp and 3.6 to 39.8 pp, measured against baselines on the same images (RQ2). A specialist API reaches 0.2428 at species level and never identifies 60.8% of the benchmark's species, while two general-purpose vision–language models reach 24 to 32% closed-set and 8.3 to 10.9% open-ended once machine-readable names are removed (RQ3). The specialist model's own confidence supports automated decisions — rejecting 85 to 97% of out-of-scope images at a 10% false-positive rate and giving 100% accuracy on the most confident quarter — where the API's does not (RQ4).

What the work adds is the measurement whose absence has kept this question open — a measurement rather than a model — with three consequences that outlive the benchmark. A pooled accuracy is a property of the composition of the test set as much as of the model, so the number moves when the specimen share moves, with nothing about identification having changed. The confound that most threatens the herbarium literature is now quantified rather than suspected, and it is large enough to reorder two systems. And the losses are attributable to identifiable factors — training support and image regime — which is what makes them actionable.

The timing matters because both conditions the audit identifies are expanding at once. Institutional digitisation is converting herbaria into image collections faster than expert review can follow, every sheet carries its species name in machine-readable print, and general-purpose vision–language models are being pointed at exactly that material. An evaluation that does not separate reading from identification will report competence that does not exist — and the diagnostic proposed in Section 10.2, a domain-asymmetric accuracy profile across the two regimes, can be run on any existing result at no cost.

For a monitoring programme the practical reading is a checklist rather than a model choice: each target species should retain training images from elsewhere, and if the intake will contain pressed or flattened material as well as living plants, both regimes must appear in training. Neither is met by collecting more images from the deployment site alone.

Two boundaries qualify the above. The backbone comparison covers one pair of models, and the mechanisms of Section 10.2 are frameworks that later work can confirm or discard rather than measurements; the numbers that answer the four questions would change first on another image set. The directions that follow — coordinates in place of regions, masked labels in place of exclusion, per-family transfer in place of aggregates — are set out in Section 10.6, together with the two observations this benchmark can explain and this paper does not.
