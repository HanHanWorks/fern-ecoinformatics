# 10. Discussion

## 10.1 Why mixed-domain and mixed-geography evaluation overstates capability

Each of the three assumptions set out in the Introduction has now been tested, and each fails. A pooled accuracy of 0.9139 describes neither image regime: the same model reaches 0.9198 on herbarium sheets and 0.9250 on field photographs when both are in training, while crossing the regimes costs 8.6 to 48.1 pp. A benchmark number is therefore a property of its composition and not only of the model — change the specimen share of the test set and the reported accuracy changes, without anything about identification having changed.

Geographic provenance is not exchangeable either, and its cost is not a constant. Holding out Taiwan, China costs 39.8 pp; holding out Vietnam costs 3.6 pp, against the same model and the same recipe. What separates the two is not distance or continent, both being in Asia, but whether the target species keep training images outside the held-out region — a Spearman correlation of 0.48 across seven countries, weak as a fitted relationship and decisive as a contrast between well-supported and poorly-supported regions.

The third assumption fails in the most consequential way. For the two vision–language models tested, apparent accuracy on herbarium material is largely an artefact of readable labels: their morphological accuracy, measured on images whose species name is not machine-readable, is 0.0825 to 0.1092. The asymmetries behind these verdicts, and the mechanisms they suggest, are taken up in Section 10.2.

## 10.2 A mechanistic account

Each verdict above admits a candidate mechanism. Three are set out here; none is proven, each makes a prediction testable without re-running these experiments, and each is stated so that it can fail.

The asymmetry between the crossing directions runs against the usual expectation: training on field photographs and testing on herbarium sheets costs 48.1 pp, the reverse only 19.9 pp, whereas if a sheet were a narrower sample of the field distribution, broader-to-narrower should be the cheaper direction. A character-based account explains the reversal. The two regimes do not show the same plant: a pressed sheet is photographed on both surfaces and exposes the ventral characters on which fern determination has always relied — sorus position and form, indusium, indument, rhizome scales — whereas a field photograph shows habit and substrate but rarely the underside of the frond. A model trained in one regime reads the characters that regime exposes and falls back on gross morphology when they are absent, so the cost of a crossing is set by how much of the diagnostic character set survives it. The prediction is that transfer cost should be largest for species separated by ventral or sporangial characters and smallest for species separable by habit; this benchmark tests it only coarsely, and it is testable sharply on new data by scoring the same species under controlled character occlusion.

The geographic result has a mechanism of a different kind. The loss does not follow from where the held-out region is: the two extremes are both in Asia, and what separates them is the share of each species' training support outside the region. A two-term account follows. The first term is coverage — a holdout removes instances, and the loss scales with the share of a species' image pool that was removed, which is why the association with out-of-region support is positive across seven countries even though it is weak. The second is composition — each region's test set carries its own specimen share, and the domain axis costs up to 48 pp, so part of every holdout drop is a domain effect in disguise. The first term is one a deployment can act on; the second is one an evaluation has to control, and no experiment here separates them. That coverage dominates is consistent with the biases documented for plant occurrence records (Meyer et al., 2016) and with the scarcity of species-level records in under-surveyed floras (Scheffers et al., 2012), and it relocates the geographic problem from cartography to data acquisition.

The third mechanism concerns the external models and has the widest reach. For a general-purpose vision–language model choosing among 152 species, the printed label on a sheet is a cue of higher reliability and lower variance than morphological inference; where the label is available, reading it maximises expected accuracy, so a model evaluated on herbarium material can appear competent while doing something else entirely. The domain asymmetry is the fingerprint of that shortcut: the model that reaches 0.379 on sheets reaches 0.069 on field photographs, and the subsets differ in text availability rather than difficulty. This yields a diagnostic that costs nothing to apply — a domain-asymmetric accuracy profile across herbarium and field material is evidence of reading until shown otherwise — and predicts that masking label regions should erase most of the apparent herbarium competence.

Two observations sit outside this account, recorded as open questions. The first is the zero-shot result: with no task-specific training, BioCLIP 2 reaches 0.6829 at species level over 152 classes — orders of magnitude above chance and well below the trained recipe — so biological pretraining encodes species-level morphology to a degree no experiment here was designed to measure; what such a model gets right and wrong without training, and whether its errors are taxonomically structured, is answerable on this benchmark and unanswered. The second is a single region: Russia has the second-thinnest out-of-region support of the eight holdouts, at 0.15, and loses only 5.6 pp, which the coverage term does not predict. The holdout is small (54 test images), so the estimate is noisy, but it is the one case in which the mechanism fails in the wrong direction, and the annotations this benchmark carries do not explain it.

## 10.3 Deployment: selective prediction and a data-acquisition checklist

The practical question a monitoring programme faces is not which model is more accurate but how to use one safely. The first mechanism is rejection. A maximum-softmax threshold on the frozen probe gives an AUROC of 0.9666 and rejects 85 to 97% of out-of-scope images at a 10% false-positive rate for most negative groups, at no additional training cost. The exception is fern-like foliage: cycads are rejected 57% of the time, a weakness shared by every model tested, so a pipeline should route fern-like non-ferns to human review rather than assume rejection handles them.

The second is selective acceptance, which the classifier's under-confidence makes safe: accepting the most confident quarter of images yields 100% accuracy, and accepting 85% still holds 0.967. The contrast with Pl@ntNet is not the one the standard summary predicts. Pl@ntNet has the lower expected calibration error of the two systems (0.090 against 0.195) and is the less usable of the two, because its most confident bin claims 0.943 and delivers 0.683. The summary statistic ranks the two systems in the opposite order to their decision-relevant behaviour: what a pipeline needs to know is not how large the calibration error is but which way it points.

Both profiles have a mechanism the literature does not predict. Guo et al. (2017) report over-confidence as the norm in modern networks, yet the frozen probe here is under-confident in every bin — a property of the linear head, which never learns to sharpen (full fine-tuning reaches an expected calibration error of 0.095 against 0.195), not of biological pretraining.

The two systems also fail in structurally different ways (Section 8): the classifier's errors stay taxonomically close, Pl@ntNet's cross family boundaries and concentrate exactly in the families where the API has no coverage at all — for a downstream user who can tolerate a wrong species but not a wrong family, that contrast matters more than the accuracy gap.

These results support a deployment recipe, but they also imply a data requirement, which is the more transferable conclusion: each target species should retain more than five training images from outside the deployment region, and if the intake will contain pressed or flattened material as well as living plants, both regimes must appear in training. Neither requirement is met by collecting more images from the deployment site alone.

## 10.4 Relationship to related work

The closest agreement in the literature is with evaluations of plant identification applications — agreement about a condition rather than a number. Pärtel et al. (2021), Rzanny et al. (2024) and Mäder et al. (2021) report high accuracy in well-sampled settings; our results agree on their own terms and locate the condition they rest on: Pl@ntNet reaches 0.799 on its best-covered family and exactly zero on eleven of the 28 families with at least five test images. The accuracy those studies measure is a property of referential coverage before it is a property of the model, and Garcin et al. (2021) named the two properties that matter: long-tailed coverage and label ambiguity.

A literature reaching the opposite conclusion about the same tools is the point of disagreement. Campbell et al. (2023), Hart et al. (2023) and Long et al. (2023) find the applications unreliable, sensitive to image framing and to who scores the outcome, and attribute the failure to the evaluation protocol; their remedies are procedural. The domain split here shows a failure no scoring convention repairs — 0.004 on herbarium sheets against 0.341 on field photographs, from a system whose ranking quality is not in question. A coverage failure is not a protocol failure.

Table 10 compares this benchmark with the three others a reader of this literature will have in mind. Pl@ntNet-300K (Garcin et al., 2021) is the reference plant image dataset: 306,146 field images over 1,081 species, difficult by design through a long-tailed class distribution and label ambiguity. PlantCLEF 2025 (Martellucci et al., 2025) shifts the difficulty to acquisition, training on about 1.4 million close-up images and testing on 2,105 expert-annotated quadrat views; Section 5 puts a number on the component their design contains. The New Guinea orchid benchmark (Saputra et al., 2026) confronts a data-poor flora with a two-stage genus-then-retrieval system. None of the three annotates image domain, performs a geographic holdout, or ships labelled negatives; this benchmark was built to carry all three, which is what makes the measurements of Sections 5 to 9 possible on one image set with one model.

On the design side, the orchid benchmark is the closest work: they evaluate genus-level identification after fine-tuning and report that general-purpose self-supervision beats biological pretraining, whereas we evaluate species-level identification with a frozen probe and find the opposite. The mechanism proposed in Section 10.2 reconciles the two and turns the disagreement into a prediction — biological pretraining carries diagnostic characters that a linear head cannot re-learn, so pretraining domain dominates while adaptation is frozen, whereas a fine-tuned trunk can recover what its pretraining lacks. Our numbers support the first half, the backbone gap of +7.1 pp being an order of magnitude larger than the adaptation-mode gap of +0.55 pp, but this benchmark tests one cell of the interaction and not the other. Work adapting a global plant model to new imagery shows the other end, what adaptation can recover (Espitalier et al., 2025, this journal); the cost measured here bounds the benefit it can be chasing.

The herbarium literature has called for measurement of exactly this kind: systematic reviews name cross-domain identification as an open problem (Hussein et al., 2022) and give explicit attention to label text and open-set conditions (Guo et al., 2025). Where those reviews describe label text as unsettled, the audit here suggests it is dominant, and the reading shortcut of Section 10.2 is a candidate explanation. In earlier work we audited five vision–language models on a much smaller 155-image set; the present audit re-runs on the full benchmark with text-leakage control, and the two sets of numbers are not comparable, because image sets, prompts and leakage treatment all differ.

The methodological instruments come from outside ecology: selective classification supplies the risk-coverage curve (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017), the calibration literature explains over-confidence (Guo et al., 2017), and open-set recognition has its own evaluation (Geng et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Botanical benchmarks have adopted neither. Applying both exposes the finding that matters for deployment: the specialist model here is worse calibrated than the commercial API in absolute terms, yet is the only one of the two whose confidence supports automated acceptance, because its errors point in the safe direction.

## 10.5 Limitations

Six limitations bound what these results support. The leave-one-region-out experiments confound removing a region with removing data: the per-holdout training sets differ in size from 2,860 to 4,088 images, and the two effects cannot be separated from these runs alone. Each country's test set also differs in image-domain composition, from 2% specimens for Taiwan, China to 81% for Thailand, so a holdout difference carries a domain component as well as a geographic one, and no experiment here estimates the interaction. The small holdouts have wide intervals — Vietnam has 28 test images and Thailand 31, roughly ±14 pp — so the argument rests on the contrast between the China-region holdouts and the well-supported ones rather than on any single country. The backbone comparison covers one pair, chosen to isolate pretraining data; a conclusion about pretraining domain drawn from two models is a claim about those two models. The mechanisms of Section 10.2 are interpretations, not measurements — consistent with the direction and size of the effects but not tested by them — and no claim in Sections 4 to 9 depends on them. A sixth limit applies to reuse: the openly licensed 24% over-represents herbarium material (63.3% specimens against 28.0% in the full set), so the field-side results and the geographic holdouts cannot be reproduced from the released images alone (Section 12). Finally, the benchmark is a snapshot without a time axis: phenological variation, from sterile fronds to spore-bearing maturity, and extreme juvenile stages are represented only incidentally, and a monitoring programme that observes a site across seasons will meet variation this benchmark does not score.

## 10.6 Future work

Three directions follow directly, and the first two test the mechanisms of Section 10.2 rather than extend the measurements. Recovering coordinates for the occurrence records would replace the region-level analysis of Section 6 with a per-species distance to the nearest training occurrence, though the country field is not sufficient and the reconstruction would need verification against the source records. Second, the label-text problem invites a controlled experiment rather than an audit: masking or removing label regions and re-measuring the models would separate reading from identification more sharply than excluding leaky images can. Third, the confusions that remain are morphological; attention maps for the errors in Section 8 would show whether the classifier is using venation and sorus characters or something incidental, and would clarify why fern-like foliage defeats rejection in every model tested.

Two further directions follow from the open questions in Section 10.2: the character-based account predicts that transfer cost varies with which diagnostic characters a species' separation rests on, testable by scoring transfer per family and by controlled character occlusion; and the zero-shot result, 0.6829 with no training, raises the question of how much species-level morphology biological pretraining already encodes and what structure its errors have.
